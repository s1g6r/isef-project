# Does ClinVar Circularly Validate the AI Tools Used to Build It? A Release-Date and Leak-Free Benchmark Test of Missense Variant Effect Predictors

## Abstract

Clinicians increasingly use AI variant effect predictors — tools that take a single amino-acid change in a protein and output a score for how likely it is to cause disease — as evidence when classifying genetic variants in ClinVar, a public database of clinical variant interpretations. Those same predictors are then benchmarked against ClinVar to measure how accurate they are. If a predictor's own output shaped the label it is later graded against, that benchmark is circular, and published accuracy numbers may not reflect how the predictor performs on a genuinely independent case.

This project tested that possibility for four predictors — AlphaMissense, ESM-1b, REVEL, and PolyPhen-2 — using a regression discontinuity design: since each predictor has a known release date, and could not have influenced any ClinVar label written before it existed, a real leakage effect should show up as a sudden jump in predictor-ClinVar agreement right at that date. Across five independent statistical checks (raw regression discontinuity, a permutation test, confounder-adjusted regression, a confounder-adjusted permutation test, and within-protein paired matching), no such jump was found for AlphaMissense or ESM-1b, the two predictors with enough surrounding ClinVar data to test the design properly. REVEL and PolyPhen-2 could not be tested conclusively — REVEL's estimate was not stable across analysis windows, and PolyPhen-2 predates ClinVar itself by three years, leaving too little "before" data.

That null result does not mean ClinVar-based accuracy is trustworthy, though. All four predictors' AUC against ClinVar exceeded their own originally published benchmarks by a meaningful margin (with one exception, discussed below). To test this a different way — one that does not depend on release dates at all — both AlphaMissense and ESM-1b were re-scored against ProteinGym, a benchmark built from laboratory deep mutational scanning experiments that could not have been seen during either predictor's training. Both predictors dropped by almost exactly the same amount, about 0.24 in AUC, on this leak-free data. A follow-up analysis found that roughly half of that drop is explained by ClinVar systematically containing easier, less ambiguous variants than ProteinGym's exhaustive mutagenesis data — but even the least ambiguous ProteinGym variants still trail ClinVar's numbers by 0.09–0.11 AUC, a gap composition alone does not account for. A second follow-up applied the same ordered-versus-disordered protein region comparison used in the release-date test to this leak-free data, and found that the gap between how well predictors do in structured versus intrinsically disordered protein regions — nearly invisible on ClinVar (about 0.01 AUC) — is six to fourteen times larger on the leak-free benchmark, meaning ClinVar substantially understates how much worse these predictors are at disordered regions specifically.

Together, these results argue against one specific circularity mechanism (a discrete jump in accuracy tied to a predictor's release) while providing strong, convergent evidence for a different problem: ClinVar-based accuracy numbers do not transfer to independent, lab-measured ground truth, for reasons that are only partly explained and remain an open question.

## 1. Introduction

A missense variant is a single change to the DNA sequence of a gene that swaps one amino acid for another in the protein it builds. Most of these changes are harmless. Some disrupt the protein badly enough to cause disease. Telling the two apart, for any specific variant a patient might carry, is one of the central problems in clinical genetics — and for the vast majority of possible variants, nobody has ever run a lab experiment to check.

Over the last several years, deep learning models have been built to fill that gap. AlphaMissense (Cheng et al., 2023), ESM-1b (Brandes et al., 2023), REVEL (Ioannidis et al., 2016), and PolyPhen-2 (Adzhubei et al., 2010) each take a variant and output a score estimating how likely it is to be pathogenic, without needing a lab experiment first. Clinicians increasingly cite these scores as supporting evidence when they submit a variant classification to ClinVar, the field's central public database of clinical variant interpretations — the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics' own guidelines (via criteria often referred to as PP3/BP4) explicitly allow computational predictions as one line of evidence in a classification.

That creates a specific risk. The standard way to measure how good one of these predictors is, is to check how well its scores agree with ClinVar's labels. But if a clinician used the predictor's own score as evidence when writing a ClinVar label in the first place, then comparing the predictor to that label is not really an independent test — the predictor is being graded, in part, against its own prior output. This project set out to test whether that circularity is actually happening, and if so, how large an effect it has on reported predictor accuracy.

Every published evaluation of these predictors that I could find treats ClinVar as clean, independent ground truth. None of them test whether the label itself could have been influenced by the tool being evaluated. That is the gap this project addresses.

## 2. Background

**Variant effect predictors.** AlphaMissense (released September 2023) combines a protein language model with structural predictions from AlphaFold. ESM-1b (August 2021) is a general-purpose protein language model — it was not trained specifically for pathogenicity prediction but has been repurposed for it. REVEL (2016) is an ensemble/meta-predictor that combines the outputs of several older tools. PolyPhen-2 (2010) is an older classifier that predates most of the modern deep learning predictors by a decade or more. These four span very different architectures and four different release years, which matters for the design below.

**Intrinsically disordered regions.** Not every part of a protein folds into a fixed three-dimensional shape. Roughly 30% of the human proteome is made up of regions that stay flexible and unstructured — intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs). Because IDRs have no fixed shape, there is less structural and functional evidence available to base a clinical judgment on, which plausibly means clinicians lean more heavily on computational predictions specifically for variants that fall in these regions. If circularity exists, it should be worse there. AlphaFold, the structure-prediction model behind AlphaMissense, produces a per-residue confidence score called pLDDT as a side effect of its structure prediction; low confidence at a position is a good proxy for that position being genuinely disordered rather than the model simply doing a bad job, since a floppy region has no single correct shape for the model to be confident about. This project used pLDDT (threshold of 70 and above for "ordered," below 50 for "disordered," following the convention used in prior IDR-focused variant effect predictor work) to identify these regions directly, rather than relying on any proxy internal to a predictor's own output.

**Prior work.** Several recent papers have found that variant effect predictors perform worse in disordered regions specifically — Lin et al. (2025, BMC Genomics) found reduced sensitivity in IDRs, with the gap largest for AlphaMissense among the tools they tested; a PLOS Computational Biology paper from August 2025 found high specificity but low sensitivity in IDRs, meaning these predictors tend to under-flag real pathogenic variants there; a bioRxiv preprint from August 2025 built a dedicated IDR-focused predictor and reported AlphaMissense's own AUC dropping from 0.94 in ordered regions to 0.85 in disordered ones. Pejaver et al. (2022, AJHG) established the ClinGen calibration thresholds that let a predictor's raw score be converted into formal ACMG evidence strength (the PP3/BP4 criteria referenced above). What none of these papers ask, and what this project is built around, is whether the ClinVar ground truth itself is independent of the predictors being scored against it.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Data and the core dataset

ClinVar's full variant summary file was filtered to GRCh38-coordinate, single-nucleotide missense variants with a Pathogenic, Likely Pathogenic, Benign, or Likely Benign classification, producing 212,903 variants after joining against AlphaMissense's genome-wide precomputed score file (DeepMind's public release, which covers essentially all possible human missense substitutions). ESM-1b scores were added from a complete precomputed catalog hosted publicly by the tool's own web portal, matched by UniProt accession and residue position (99.9% of variants matched). REVEL and PolyPhen-2 scores, along with gnomAD population allele frequencies used later as a confounder control, were added from dbNSFP, a database that bundles scores from many predictors on a shared genomic coordinate system (matched for 99.3% and 92.6% of variants respectively). AlphaFold structure files were downloaded for every unique protein in the dataset (14,930 of 15,144 needed accessions, 98.6%), and per-residue confidence scores (pLDDT) were parsed directly from each structure file to classify every variant's position as ordered, disordered, or an intermediate zone excluded from the ordered-vs-disordered comparison. 31.1% of annotated variants fell in disordered regions, closely matching the literature's expected proteome-wide rate of roughly 30% — a check that gave confidence the disorder annotation was measuring something real rather than an artifact.

A separate table recording each variant's earliest appearance in any ClinVar release (rather than its most recent review date) was built from 18 archival ClinVar snapshots spanning 2018–2026, to guard against a specific confound: a variant's "last evaluated" date can be updated by an unrelated re-review long after its original classification, which would make an old classification look artificially recent in a naive date-based analysis.

### 3.2 Release-date discontinuity test

The core test looks for a sudden jump in how often a predictor's own call matches the ClinVar label, right at that predictor's release date — the logic being that a predictor cannot have influenced a label written before it existed, so any real leakage effect should produce a discontinuity, not a gradual trend. This was implemented as a regression discontinuity design: for each predictor, a linear regression of match/no-match outcome against time was fit separately on data just before and just after the release date, with the coefficient on a before/after indicator giving the size of any jump. This was run at three window widths (1, 2, and 3 years on either side of the release date) for each predictor, since a real discontinuity should show up consistently across nearby window choices rather than only appearing at one specific width.

Because a single regression's p-value assumes the true relationship really is a straight line near the cutoff — an assumption that will not always hold — every result that looked significant was cross-checked with a permutation test: rerunning the identical procedure at 2,000 fake "release dates" drawn from the predictor's own real data, and checking whether the real jump was actually unusual relative to that distribution, or just an ordinary result of chance.

A confounder-adjusted version of the same regression added review status, submitter count, and log allele frequency (with a missing-value indicator, since being entirely absent from population frequency databases is itself informative) as covariates, to check whether the null results held up after accounting for more mundane explanations. A separate, non-regression check — matching each variant just after a release date to the nearest variant in the same protein just before it, then running McNemar's test on the paired outcomes — controlled for gene/protein identity a different way, without needing a covariate for every one of the thousands of genes involved.

### 3.3 Leak-free benchmark comparison

The release-date test only rules out one specific mechanism: a sudden jump in accuracy tied to when a predictor became available. It cannot detect a subtler form of inflation, such as ClinVar simply being an easier dataset by composition than the space of all possible variants. To test that possibility, AlphaMissense and ESM-1b were separately scored against ProteinGym, a public collection of deep mutational scanning (DMS) experiments — laboratory measurements of how a specific mutation affects protein function, obtained directly rather than through clinical interpretation. Because these measurements come from wet-lab experiments rather than clinician judgment informed by computational tools, they could not have been circularly influenced by either predictor. Ninety-six human-protein DMS assays were used, restricted to single-amino-acid substitutions to keep the comparison identical across both predictors (AlphaMissense cannot score multi-mutant combinations). ESM-1b's scores came from ProteinGym's own precomputed baseline results; AlphaMissense scores were cross-referenced from the same genome-wide score file used for the ClinVar analysis, matched by protein and substitution rather than genomic coordinate (this join was verified by manually checking that 30 randomly sampled matches had the correct reference amino acid at the claimed protein position, all 30 of which checked out).

## 4. Results

### 4.1 No release-date discontinuity, but a real accuracy excess

AlphaMissense showed no discontinuity at any of the three window widths tested (jump estimates between +0.0017 and +0.0046, none statistically significant), and the permutation test confirmed the same conclusion at every width (p between 0.75 and 0.92). ESM-1b initially looked more promising — a statistically significant jump at the narrowest window (+0.0237, p=0.0024) — but the permutation test placed that same result well within the range expected by chance (permutation p=0.32), and a separate check found that AlphaMissense's own retrospective accuracy, even though the tool did not exist until two years later, peaked in the exact same calendar month as ESM-1b's apparent jump. That ruled out ESM-1b's release as the actual cause: the real explanation was an unusually large, mostly-benign batch of new ClinVar submissions that month, spread across many genes rather than concentrated in one — benign-heavy batches are generally easier for any predictor to score correctly, inflating accuracy for reasons that have nothing to do with either tool.

REVEL and PolyPhen-2 could not be evaluated conclusively with this design. REVEL's jump estimate flipped sign depending on the window width used and only reached significance at the widest window tested — a pattern that argues against trusting it, the same way a real physical effect should not appear or disappear depending on how far you zoom out. PolyPhen-2's test was underpowered for a structural reason rather than a statistical fluke: the tool was published in 2010, three years before ClinVar itself existed, leaving almost no real "before" data to compare against.

Adding confounder controls (review status, submitter count, allele frequency) and a completely different non-regression method (within-protein paired matching) did not change the picture for AlphaMissense or ESM-1b — both stayed null across every version of the test. One subtlety worth noting: the confounder-adjusted regression initially produced two p-values under 0.05 that the raw regression had not shown, and it would have been easy to report those as a new finding. Running them through the same permutation check applied everywhere else showed both were not actually unusual (permutation p=0.58 and 0.35) — a reminder that adding covariates to a regression does not by itself make its p-values any more trustworthy.

Despite this comprehensive null result, three of the four predictors' AUC against ClinVar exceeded their own originally published benchmark by a meaningful margin: AlphaMissense at 0.9592 against a published range of roughly 0.90–0.93, REVEL at 0.9670 against a published range of roughly 0.90–0.96, and PolyPhen-2 (using its HVAR-tuned variant) at 0.9169 against a published range of roughly 0.80–0.88. The exception is worth keeping rather than glossing over: PolyPhen-2's HDIV-tuned variant, at 0.8973, actually fell inside its own published range. Four architecturally unrelated predictors is a wide enough sample that this pattern is unlikely to be one model's individual quirk — but a release-date jump was specifically ruled out as the explanation, which meant the excess needed a different kind of test.

### 4.2 A leak-free benchmark reveals a large, consistent gap

On ProteinGym, AlphaMissense's AUC dropped from 0.9592 (ClinVar) to 0.7164, and ESM-1b's dropped from 0.9299 to 0.6906 — drops of 0.2428 and 0.2393 respectively, nearly identical despite the two models having very different architectures. That similarity across two unrelated model designs is itself notable: it suggests whatever is driving the gap is a property of the benchmark or the underlying prediction problem, not something specific to one model's training process.

This gap does not, by itself, prove training-data leakage — the release-date test already argued against a sudden leakage event, and a gap this size is also consistent with ClinVar simply being an easier benchmark by composition, since clinicians tend to only finalize a classification once there is fairly convincing evidence either way, while ProteinGym's deep mutational scanning assays measure every possible substitution at every position, ambiguous ones included. A follow-up check tested one version of this idea directly: restricting ClinVar to only its highest-confidence labels (variants reviewed by an expert panel or covered by a formal practice guideline) did not raise the AUC — both predictors actually did slightly worse on that subset. The likely reason is that expert-panel review measures how well-vetted a label is, not how biologically clear-cut the underlying variant is; a variant often gets pulled into formal review specifically because it was contested or clinically important, not because it was routine.

A second, more direct follow-up measured each ProteinGym variant's distance from its own assay's median fitness score — a variant right at the median is about as biologically ambiguous as a measured effect gets, while one at either extreme is unambiguous — and computed AUC separately across ten groups from least to most extreme. Both predictors showed the same pattern: accuracy rose steadily from barely above chance (about 0.51–0.52 AUC) among the most ambiguous variants to 0.81–0.87 AUC among the most extreme ones, then flattened rather than continuing to climb. This is real, substantial evidence that ClinVar's composition — its tendency to under-represent ambiguous, borderline cases — explains a large share of the original gap. But even at the single most extreme, least ambiguous decile, both predictors still trailed their ClinVar numbers by 0.09–0.11 AUC. If composition explained the entire gap, that remaining difference should have closed almost completely, and it did not. The honest conclusion is that composition-difficulty explains roughly half of the original 0.24 AUC gap, with a real, bounded remainder left unexplained — possibly reflecting that deep mutational scanning fitness and clinical pathogenicity are related but distinct measurements of "how bad is this variant," a subtler form of leakage than a release-date test is built to detect, or a difference between the specific proteins ProteinGym happens to cover and the disease-gene-heavy set ClinVar represents.

### 4.3 ClinVar substantially understates the ordered-vs-disordered performance gap

The last analysis applied the ordered-versus-disordered structural comparison — originally built to test whether circularity concentrates in disordered regions — to the leak-free ProteinGym data, to see whether a gap that looked small on ClinVar would look different on data that was not filtered by clinical curation at all.

| | AlphaMissense | ESM-1b |
|---|---|---|
| ClinVar, ordered regions | 0.9530 AUC (n=111,445) | 0.9143 AUC (n=111,317) |
| ClinVar, disordered regions | 0.9420 AUC (n=57,158) | 0.9071 AUC (n=57,103) |
| ClinVar gap | 0.0111 | 0.0072 |
| ProteinGym, ordered regions | 0.7078 AUC (n=76,760) | 0.6789 AUC (n=266,645) |
| ProteinGym, disordered regions | 0.5630 AUC (n=9,459) | 0.6149 AUC (n=38,340) |
| ProteinGym gap | 0.1448 | 0.0640 |

On ClinVar, ordered and disordered regions look almost equally predictable — a difference of one AUC point or less for either predictor. On the leak-free benchmark, the gap is six to fourteen times larger. AlphaMissense's AUC on disordered ProteinGym variants, 0.563, is barely better than a coin flip — a genuinely weak result that the ClinVar-based numbers gave no indication of. The most likely explanation mirrors the composition story above: ClinVar rarely resolves a truly ambiguous disordered-region variant into a firm pathogenic-or-benign call in the first place, since there is less structural evidence available to support a confident classification, so its disordered-region bucket ends up nearly as "easy," on average, as its ordered-region bucket. ProteinGym's exhaustive mutagenesis has no such filter, so the predictors' real weakness in disordered regions comes through without that dilution.

This result should not be read as proof that these predictors are fundamentally bad at disordered regions in some general sense — ProteinGym's 96 human proteins were chosen for practical reasons (whether a deep mutational scanning experiment was feasible to run on them), not to represent the same disease-relevant population ClinVar covers, so part of the gap could reflect which specific disordered regions happen to appear in this particular benchmark rather than disorder itself. What is solid is narrower but still meaningful: the ordered-versus-disordered gap is real and substantial on the one benchmark that could not have leaked into training, and ClinVar's version of that same comparison is not a reliable stand-in for it.

## 5. Discussion

Two different findings need to be held separately, because a paper on this topic is easy to overclaim in either direction. The release-date test, run five independent ways across two predictors with sufficient data, found no evidence of a sudden discontinuity in accuracy tied to when a predictor became available. That is a real, carefully verified negative result — it specifically rules out training-data leakage that would produce a sudden jump, one plausible and testable mechanism for circularity. It does not, on its own, establish that ClinVar-based accuracy is trustworthy.

The leak-free benchmark comparison shows the opposite side of that coin: a large, consistent, and reproducible gap between ClinVar-based and lab-measured accuracy for both predictors tested, with roughly half of that gap traceable to ClinVar under-representing biologically ambiguous variants, and a real remainder that composition alone does not explain. The disordered-region follow-up adds a second, independently derived data point pointing the same direction — ClinVar's structural-region comparison and ProteinGym's structural-region comparison tell noticeably different stories, and the leak-free one is the one with no possible clinical-curation filter behind it.

Put together, the most defensible conclusion this project supports is not "AlphaMissense is circularly validated" in the sense originally hypothesized — the release-date evidence argues against that specific, sudden-jump version of the story. It is closer to: ClinVar-based accuracy numbers, for at least two structurally different predictors, do not transfer to an independent benchmark nearly as well as the published numbers suggest, for reasons that are only partly understood, and clinicians and researchers citing a ClinVar-derived AUC as evidence of real-world performance should treat that number with real caution rather than at face value.

### Limitations

Several things this project does not establish, stated directly rather than left implicit:

- **REVEL and PolyPhen-2 were not conclusively tested** by the release-date design. REVEL's result was unstable across analysis windows and is reported as inconclusive rather than a finding either way; PolyPhen-2 predates ClinVar itself, leaving too little data to test the design at all. Extending the leak-free comparison to these two predictors, rather than only AlphaMissense and ESM-1b, is a natural next step.
- **A bonus hypothesis was never implemented.** The original plan included testing whether the release-date jump (or lack of one) changes when restricting to ClinVar variants that have documented non-computational evidence on record, parsed from ClinVar's full XML submission data rather than the summary file used throughout this project. That parsing work was never started; it remains a real, testable extension rather than a completed negative result.
- **Deep mutational scanning fitness and clinical pathogenicity are related but not identical measurements.** A variant can reduce a protein's function in a lab assay without necessarily causing human disease, and the reverse is possible too. Some of the unexplained AUC remainder in the leak-free comparison could reflect this construct mismatch rather than anything about predictor quality or leakage.
- **ProteinGym's protein selection differs from ClinVar's.** The 96 human proteins used were chosen because a deep mutational scanning experiment was practically feasible on them, not to match ClinVar's disease-gene-heavy composition. This applies to both the overall AUC comparison and the disordered-region comparison, and means neither result should be generalized to "all human proteins" without caution.
- **AlphaMissense's leak-free numbers rest on partial coverage.** Only 28% of ProteinGym's variants had a matching AlphaMissense score, versus effectively complete coverage for ESM-1b. A manual spot-check of 30 random matches confirmed the join itself is correct, which gives real confidence the 28% subset is not biased by a coordinate-mapping bug, but it remains a smaller sample than ESM-1b's.
- **The remaining ~0.09–0.11 AUC gap after controlling for composition-difficulty is not explained** by any analysis in this project. It is reported honestly as an open question rather than attributed to a specific cause.

### Future work

The most promising next steps, in rough order of how directly they follow from what has already been built: implementing the ClinVar-XML evidence-code analysis that was originally planned but never started; extending the leak-free ProteinGym comparison to REVEL and PolyPhen-2, now that the pipeline for AlphaMissense and ESM-1b exists; and directly investigating what the remaining unexplained AUC gap actually reflects — starting with whether it is concentrated in specific proteins, specific types of amino acid substitution, or specific DMS assay types, rather than spread evenly, which the current analysis has not checked.

## 6. Conclusion

Testing four widely used AI variant effect predictors for a specific, discrete form of circular validation — a sudden accuracy jump tied to a predictor's own release date — found no evidence of it for the two predictors well-suited to the test, across five independent statistical methods. But a separate, leak-free comparison against laboratory-measured ground truth found that ClinVar-based accuracy substantially overstates real-world predictive performance for both predictors tested, by an amount only partly explained by ClinVar's tendency to contain easier, less ambiguous variants — and that ClinVar specifically hides a large weakness in how these predictors perform on intrinsically disordered protein regions, a weakness with real clinical relevance given that disordered-region variants are exactly the cases where a clinician has the least other evidence to fall back on. The field's standard practice of citing a ClinVar-derived AUC as a predictor's real-world accuracy does not appear to be well supported by this data, regardless of whether the underlying mechanism is training-data leakage in some subtler form, benchmark composition, or something not yet identified.
